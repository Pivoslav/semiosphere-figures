How this figure is made

Each panel opens one step on the way from the report file to the picture, with the equation it uses.

The gate rule when an item stops before any model is called
gate(q) = R1(Hq) ∨ R2(Hq) ∨ R3(Hq)

For question q, Hq is the metadata on the five documents the retriever returned. The gate closes if any of three rules fires. R1 and R2 fire when an unpaired outward claim or a documented miss appears anywhere among the five. R3 is the prompt's halt clause rewritten as a rule. No language model is involved. On the flat retriever the gate closed on 11 of 18 items: R1 on 7, R2 on 3, R3 on 1.

Counting generator calls where 36 and 14 come from
Carm = 2 · ngen,   ngen = 18 − nclosed

Every item that reaches the generator is handled in two hops, so it costs two calls. In B2 nothing closes: 2 × 18 = 36. In D2 eleven items close: 2 × 7 = 14, which saves 22 calls.

Scoring the refusals the measures on the 2D page
recall = TP / (TP + FN),   false refusal = FP / 13,   precision = TP / (TP + FP),   F1 = 2 · precision · recall / (precision + recall)
SymbolStands for
TPRefusals on the 5 items that should be refused.
FNShould-refuse items that got an answer instead.
FPRefusals on the 13 items that should be answered.

Worked through for D2: TP = 5 and FP = 8, so recall is 5/5 = 1.0, false refusal is 8/13 = 0.6154, precision is 5/13 = 0.3846, and F1 is 0.5556. Six of those eight false refusals came from the gate and two from the generator. For B2: TP = 1 and FP = 5, giving recall 0.2, false refusal 0.3846, precision 0.1667 and F1 0.1818.

From counts to the picture blocks, paths and particles

The retriever, gate and generator sit in a row at x = −4.5, −0.5 and 3.5, with the halt block below the gate. An item that reaches the generator follows a path that loops once over it (the second hop) and exits to the right. A stopped item drops from the gate into the halt block. Particle i starts at the fraction i/18 of its path, so all eighteen are spread out before you press Play.

ui ← (ui + 0.11 Δt) mod 1,   position = path(ui)

Δt is the time since the last frame in seconds, and path(u) is the point a fraction u of the way along the path by length. \1 Turn the model spins the scene slowly; nothing moves until you press Play or Turn.

Where the numbers and code live

All counts come from ma4b_rule_layer_halt_report.json (llama3.2:3b, temperature 0.0, seed 19870317, flat top-5 retrieval). The 2D page lists the report key for each one. The scoped follow-up is in ma4c_scoped_rule_gate_report.json. The drawing code is the script at the bottom of this page, using assets/fig3d.js and Three.js r128.

In plain words

What you are looking at

  • The pale green block is the retriever. For each question it fetches the five archive documents that look most relevant.
  • The red block is the rule gate. It reads the labels on those five documents, the way a clerk checks stamps on a file, and decides whether a licensed answer is possible.
  • The orange block is the generator, the language model that writes the answer.
  • The dark green block underneath is the standard refusal sentence the pipeline prints when the gate says no.
  • Each dot is one of 18 test questions. Five of them should be refused and thirteen should be answered.

What it shows

Without a gate (B2), every question goes to the model twice, 36 calls in all, and the model catches only one of the five questions it should refuse. With the gate (D2), eleven questions stop before any model sees them, the model runs only fourteen times, and all five should-refuse questions are caught. The gate is not precise, though. Six of the eleven it stopped could have been answered. With a better retriever in the follow-up (MA4c), it stopped none at all.

Why it matters

The dissertation reads the boundary between secret and public Soviet texts as the place where meaning is governed: what may cross, in what form, and what must be refused. In Lotman's terms the boundary is a filter. The KGB memo's hostile wording never reached the embassy release, because something at the boundary stopped it.

This figure asks where that decision should sit in an AI pipeline. When the refusal rule was written as a sentence in the model's prompt (arm C2, on the 2D page), the model refused all eighteen questions, including ones its own instructions said to answer. The rule was never evaluated as a condition. Put in front of the model as plain logic, the same rule kept every correct refusal and cut the calls by more than half. For anyone building with AI, the lesson is to keep boundary rules in the plumbing, at least with small models, and not to trust the model to police itself.

Potential for the bigger picture

The most practically useful result on the site, and the one most likely to matter outside it. The negative half is decisive: a refusal sentence in a small model's prompt is close to useless as a safeguard, because the model refused everything. The positive half needs more work. The gate is topic blind, so on flat retrieval it stops answerable questions, and on the scoped retriever it stops nothing at all.

Eighteen items and one model are thin, and one item currently moves recall by 0.20. The next steps are already sketched in open thread T28: a halt at the retrieval layer that reads which documents were touched, combined with scoped context. Beyond that, a larger frozen item set with more should-refuse cases, and a second model family to check that the prompt-clause failure isn't a quirk of one 3B model. For the dissertation, this is also the first measured case of a code handed to a receiver making it less discerning, the reverse of Lotman's colour example (T27). It deserves a deliberately designed positive case alongside it.
