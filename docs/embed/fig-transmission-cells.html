Transmission in Lotman: cells, verbs, outcomes

Lotman is often quoted as if he hated the word transmission. He did hate it as a picture of meaning moving like a parcel from sender to receiver. He did not hate it as a picture of codes crossing between parts of culture. These three figures are a cheat sheet for that distinction, plus one awkward square on a chart where our own measurements landed. Grey dashed boxes are Lotman's categories. The solid blue box and the red/green numbers are ours.

Jump: T1 · the four rooms · T2 · three verbs · T3 · enlarge vs collapse · How this ties to the journal · Read order for the committee

Why this page exists (plain version)

The dissertation asks whether multi-agent pipelines behave like dialogue between independent voices, like one institution talking to itself (autocommunication), or like something Lotman's typology never named. Transmission is the wrong word for most of that story. These figures say which wrong word people keep reaching for, and where the right one might live.

If the committee's eyes glaze over at "semiotic heterogeneity," tell them: Lotman thinks cultures need at least two languages that do not fully translate, and that pretending one pipe carries meaning unchanged is how you accidentally write a Globe and Mail article into an embassy packet.

Figure T1 · The cells, and the one that has no name

How to read T1 without the jargon

Imagine a spreadsheet with two questions. Did the message get bigger or smaller? and Did the receiver bring their own stuff? Lotman puts ordinary outward mail in the "same size, receiver adds little" corner (I-s/he). He puts internal pep-talk in the "gets bigger, but only rhythm and repetition do the work" corner (I-I). He puts reflex arcs in the basement: parts of one organism, not a conversation.

The blue dot is us. Parent agent delegates to subagent; the reply comes back longer and mostly new words. That is not "mail unchanged" and not "pep-talk with no new facts." It is a fourth room Lotman never labelled. The journal's section 13 argues the dissertation should admit the room exists before it names it.

Figure T2 · What actually crosses, texts or codes

Three lanes, one allowed use of "transmission"

Think of two departments in a university that share a building but not a vocabulary. When they talk, three different things happen and English uses one sloppy verb for all of them.

Texts get translated: something always falls off the truck. Meaning is generated in the gap between non-identical codes: that is where new thought lives, and it is not a parcel. Codes and metatexts can pass intact: grammar rules, citation formats, the typed hand-off envelope from experiment MA1. Only the bottom lane earns the word transmission in Lotman's strict sense.

Committee translation: "We were not measuring meaning-in-a-tube. We may have been measuring whether a rule sticker survived the tube."

Three verbs, three lanes, one correct use of the word transmission
texts · translated, part always cut off meaning · generated, never crosses whole codes and metatexts · transmitted, strict sense
an exchange of metatexts, of codes, which are transmitted from one "hemisphere" of culture to another

Theory tie-in: Sections 8 and 10 worry about bilingual relay at the archive boundary. T2 says the engineering mistake is to describe all three lanes with one verb. Interpretation tip: orange and purple lanes are where novelty and loss belong; green is where compliance belongs, and where a halt rule should live if anywhere.

Figure T3 · A transmitted code can enlarge or collapse the receiver

Same gift, opposite birthday

Left side (grey, Lotman's story): culture hands you a finer colour vocabulary. After the code crosses, you notice shades you could not name before. That is enlargement: the receiver can evaluate the code and see more.

Right side (measured, MA4b): we handed a small model a halt instruction in its prompt. It treated the instruction like a blanket ban and refused everything, including items whose own message already showed the tags that should have meant "you may answer." That is collapse: the receiver could only pattern-match, not read the condition.

The rule layer upstream (same predicate, no model in the loop) kept recall but cut false refusals. Moral for the dissertation: codes are not neutral pipes. They are tests of whether the receiver has a position.

Same act of code transfer, opposite effects on what the receiver can tell apart
enlargement · receiver gains a distinction collapse · receiver loses a distinction grey text · unmeasured, Lotman's example

Theory tie-in: journal section 13 uses this pair to argue that "move halt upstream" is not enough: once a gate works, remove the prompt clause or it vandalises cleared items. Section 15 (MA10) adds the darker cousin: typed envelopes can launder invention if the parent stamps real ids on fiction. T3's left half is aspirational; the right half is what we actually measured on a 3B model.
