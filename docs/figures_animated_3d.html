Animated 3D figures

These are the 3D companions to the experiment charts. Each one takes a number we already measured and gives it a shape you can turn around, like a loop that fails to close or a gate that stops a question before any model sees it. The shapes are teaching aids. If a 3D view ever disagrees with its 2D chart, the 2D chart and the report file behind it win, and the 3D view has a bug.

How to read any of these

Drag to turn the scene and scroll to zoom. Where there is a Play button, the motion stands for a process: an item moving through a pipeline, or a passage going out and coming back. Scenes without one are structures you look at from different sides. If your system is set to reduce motion, nothing plays on its own.

Every card ends with a line saying which parts are measured and which are drawn for the eye. Words with a dotted underline open a short definition. Click the word a second time to jump to its full entry in the journal glossary. Phrases with a gold dashed underline open the source behind the claim. Names of journal sections, figures, experiments and open threads have a thin solid underline and open a short plain-language note on what that part of the project is.

Each figure page ends with two extra sections. "How this figure is made" has fold-out panels with the equations and constants used to draw it. "In plain words" explains everything in the picture, why it matters, and how much potential the angle has.

Which angles are worth pursuing

An honest scorecard

Worth building on now. The Montreal semiosphere is the core of the dissertation, because every arc rests on two documents read side by side. The operator counts behind it are large and one-sided. The halt result is the site's clearest practical lesson for anyone building with AI. The round trips show cleanly that changing the audience moves text much further than changing the model.

Promising, but short of data, so be patient with them. The explosion rests on two early memos and needs more from before 1983. The model pairings are four cells of a matrix that is cheap to finish. The rooms and the delegation stack rest on one agent's logs and need other kinds of exchange placed beside them. Each page ends with specific suggestions.

Dead ends, at least as evidence. Embedding geometry as proof of the boundary has failed twice and was withdrawn. A round-trip score on its own can't tell creation from damage. Content gain can't show a contribution when the sources are hidden. Isolating a subagent produced invention, not a second voice. A refusal sentence in a small model's prompt made it refuse everything. These are worth reporting as negative results, not repeating.

Where to start

If you only have five minutes

Open the delegation stack and the transmission room together. The first shows that no hand-off got shorter. The second shows where that leaves the parent and subagent in Lotman's scheme, which is in a room he never drew.

Then open the halt flow. It is the one with a practical lesson: put the refusal rule upstream of the model, because a sentence in the prompt asking the model to refuse got treated as a ban on everything.

The Lotman L1 to L3 models are the archive side of the argument. They read best in the gallery, where all four sit on one page.
